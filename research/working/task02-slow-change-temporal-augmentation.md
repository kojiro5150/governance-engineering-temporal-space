## Central question

Can an instrument make relationships between gradual changes recognisable when neither change is readily noticed as it unfolds?

Improved recognition is not assumed to be a new perceptual capacity.

## Verification key

- **[V]** Bibliographic details confirmed against a located source in this task.
- **[P]** Partly verified: title, venue or DOI confirmed; some details (authors, pages, design) from secondary listings or memory.
- **[U]** Unverified.

Evidence types:

- **Direct experimental evidence:** a reported experiment with a measured outcome.
- **Theoretical interpretation:** an author's or reviewer's account of findings.
- **My interpretation:** how I read the evidence for this question.
- **Speculation:** not supported by sources reviewed.

Design details for most studies are taken from abstracts and author pages, not full texts. Where a full text was not read, the entry says so.

## Part 1. Targeted literature review

### 1.1 Detection thresholds for gradual and slow-onset changes

**Simons, D. J., Franconeri, S. L., & Reimer, R. L. (2000).** Change blindness in the absence of a visual disruption. *Perception*, 29(10), 1143–1154. doi:10.1068/p3104 [V]
- Design: two experiments; observers watched scenes continuously while a large change occurred gradually, with no disruption. Intentional and incidental conditions.
- Finding (direct): large gradual changes went undetected; the gradual change signal did not appear to draw attention. The authors note that very slow natural changes (hair or grass growing) fall below the threshold for perception.
- Limitations: change durations of seconds; static scenes; no parametric manipulation of rate reported in the sources I read.
- Relevance: establishes that continuous availability of information does not ensure detection. Detection failure is attributed to absence of an attention-capturing transient.

**David, E., Laloyaux, C., Devue, C., & Cleeremans, A. (2006).** Change blindness to gradual changes in facial expressions. *Psychologica Belgica*, 46(4), 253–268. doi:10.5334/pb-46-4-253 [V]
- Design: gradual versus disrupted changes to facial expressions and to object colour within realistic scenes.
- Finding (direct): gradual changes were detected about three times less often than disrupted ones; about 15% of observers detected the gradual change in a trial. Face changes were detected better than colour changes on non-face objects.
- Relevance: stimulus relevance modulates but does not eliminate gradual change blindness.

**Laloyaux, C., et al. (c. 2008).** Implicit influence of undetected gradual facial-expression changes. PubMed 17446088 [P]
- Finding (direct, from abstract): observers who failed to detect the gradual change were still influenced by it in later recognition decisions and showed lower confidence.
- Relevance: "not noticed" does not mean "not registered". Any instrument study must separate explicit report from implicit influence.

**Marples, D., Carter, P., Gledhill, D., & Goodson, S. (2019/2020).** Broad environmental change blindness in virtual environments and video games. In *Videogame Sciences and Arts* (conference proceedings, Springer) [P]
- Design: 119 participants solving an escape-room game while surface colour and texture changed gradually over 90, 45, 22.5 or 11.25 s.
- Finding (direct): about 4.2% detected the changes; no significant effect of change duration on detection.
- Limitations: incidental task, game context, conference paper.
- Relevance: within roughly 11 to 90 s, rate did not predict detection in this setting. This argues against a simple rate threshold model, at least under incidental viewing.

**Method paper on slow-change-blindness stimulus generation (NYU He lab)** [P]
- Theoretical statement: the authors describe slow change blindness as under-researched and the stimulus supply as a limiting factor.
- Relevance: supports the view that the parametric psychophysics of slow change (threshold as a function of rate, magnitude and attention) is not established.

**Gap identified (my interpretation).** I found no source giving a psychometric function for detecting gradual change as a function of rate under intentional viewing. This is a scan finding, not evidence that such work does not exist.

### 1.2 Perception of rates of change and relationships between changing processes

**Rate and acceleration**

**Werkhoven, P., Snippe, H. P., & Toet, A. (1992).** Visual processing of optic acceleration. *Vision Research*, 32, 2313 onward [P: volume and start page from the article scan; end page unverified]
- Design: detection of velocity modulations of moving dots and patterns.
- Finding (direct, theoretical reading by authors): detection of velocity modulation is not directly based on an acceleration signal.

**Watamaniuk, S. N. J., & Heinen, S. J. (2003).** Perceptual and oculomotor evidence of limitations on processing accelerating motion. *Journal of Vision*, 3(11), 698–709. doi:10.1167/3.11.5 [V]
- Design: observers pursued or judged targets with random base speeds and subsequent accelerations.
- Finding (direct): thresholds for both pursuit and perception were higher for acceleration than for speed.

**Nakayama, R., & Motoyoshi, I. (2017).** Sensitivity to acceleration in the human early visual system. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 8, 925. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00925 [P]
- Competing finding: argues the visual system is directly sensitive to acceleration at a preattentive level, contrary to the prevailing view.
- Relevance: rate-of-change perception is contested even for visible motion. None of these studies addresses rate change in slow, non-motion drift (for example, luminance or hue).

**Relationships between processes: synchrony, binding, order**

**Holcombe, A. O. (2009).** Seeing slow and seeing fast: Two limits on perception. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 13(5), 216–221. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2009.02.005 [V]
- Review (theoretical interpretation of many experiments): visual temporal limits fall into a fast group (motion direction, depth, edges) and a slow, temporally coarse group, including pairing of colour and motion and joint identification of separated features; the slow group may reflect high-level processes.
- Relevance: judging a relationship between two changing things (which changed with which, which came first) belongs to the slow group. This predicts that compressing time too far will degrade relational judgements even as it improves detection.

**Fujisaki, W., & Nishida, S. (2005).** Temporal frequency characteristics of synchrony–asynchrony discrimination of audio-visual signals. *Experimental Brain Research*, 166, 455–464 [V: from authors' publication list]
- Finding (direct, as reported in later papers): synchrony–asynchrony discrimination for repetitive audio-visual stimuli fails above about 4 Hz. Related conference work by the same authors found similar limits for audio-visual and visuo-tactile pairs and higher limits for audio-tactile pairs [P].
- Relevance: relational temporal judgements have a low ceiling on rate, supporting Holcombe's slow-limit account.

**Martini, P. (2013).** Sources of bias and uncertainty in a visual temporal individuation task. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*, 75(1), 168–181. doi:10.3758/s13414-012-0384-y [V]
- Finding (direct, from abstract): serial order estimation used a noisy temporal rather than ordinal code; attentional filtering improved categorisation but not temporal precision. The author proposes an internal temporal sampling instability acting as a low-pass filter on order judgement.
- Relevance: if order precision is limited by a sampling instability independent of attention, an instrument that only directs attention may not improve order judgement. It must change the timescale or the representation.

**Order judgement for gradual onsets (closest prior art found)**
- Pastore and colleagues, *Perception & Psychophysics* (1988), 44, 257–271 [P: cited by the author in a 1992 mailing-list post; not read]. Reported that for abrupt auditory onsets, order threshold is independent of rise time, but for gradual onsets it depends on rise time.
- Jaśkowski, P. (1993). Temporal-order judgment and reaction time to stimuli of different rise times. *Perception*, 22 [P: sage record found; pages unverified]. Suggests observers use events beyond the physical onset when judging order.
- Jaśkowski, P. (1991). Perceived onset simultaneity of stimuli with unequal durations. *Perception*, 20, 715–726 [P]. Observers took offset asynchrony into account despite instructions to judge onsets.
- Relevance (my interpretation): order judgement of gradual onsets has been studied for rise times of milliseconds to perhaps hundreds of milliseconds. I found no study of onset order for changes rising over seconds to tens of seconds. Jaśkowski's offset finding is a direct design warning: if ramps end at different times, observers may judge by offsets.

### 1.3 Temporal compression, time-lapse and perceptual integration

**Steinhof, V., Schroeger, A., Liepelt, R., & Sperl, L. (2025).** Time and video speed perception: a comprehensive investigation of the relation between estimated video speed, clip duration and original duration. *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*, 10(1), 36. doi:10.1186/s41235-025-00637-2 [V]
- Finding (direct, from author summary): viewers underestimate the speed of time-lapse and overestimate the speed of slow motion; authors interpret this as assimilation toward an internal default reference.
- Related: Sperl and colleagues report in conference material that explicit speed information can partly mitigate these biases [P, conference abstract only]. Sperl, L., & Liepelt, R. Human perception of altered video speed. *Timing & Time Perception* [P, in press at time of listing].
- Relevance: time-lapse distorts magnitude judgements (speed, duration). Ordinal relations (which came first) are invariant under uniform compression. A pilot that measures order is protected from these biases; one that measures rate or duration is not.

**Accelerated playback studies** [P]
- An eye-tracking study of news videos (N = 146) found accelerated playback associated with lower perceived comprehension and poorer memory (PMC record, authors not confirmed).
- A three-experiment study in *Frontiers in Psychology* (2025, provisionally accepted) found faster playback increased perceived distortion and modestly reduced satisfaction, with limited effects on engagement.
- Relevance: these concern speech-led media at 1.5 to 2x, not slow processes compressed 10 to 100x. They caution that compression has costs, not only benefits.

**Pöppel, E. (1997).** A hierarchical model of temporal perception. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 1(2), 56–61 [V, from prior scan]
- Theoretical: an integration window of roughly two to three seconds.
- My interpretation: compression may help by bringing a slow relation inside this window. This is a hypothesis, not a finding.

**Tversky, B., Morrison, J. B., & Bétrancourt, M. (2002).** Animation: Can it facilitate? *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, 57(4), 247–262 [V, from prior scan]
- Review: animation often fails to beat informationally equivalent static graphics.
- Relevance: the strongest existing reason to expect condition C (static representation) to match or beat condition B (compressed replay).

### 1.4 Sensory augmentation and temporal augmentation

**König, S. U., Schumann, F., Keyser, J., Goeke, C., et al., & König, P. (2016).** Learning new sensorimotor contingencies. *PLOS ONE*, 11(12), e0166647 [V, from prior scan]
- Design: seven weeks of training with a vibrotactile belt signalling magnetic north.
- Finding (direct): physiological and subjective changes compatible with acquiring new sensorimotor contingencies; a related report found the belt facilitated navigation and new strategies [P].
- Relevance: the best evidence in either scan that an instrument can produce perception-like capacity. It concerns spatial orientation, required weeks of active use, and coupled the signal to the user's own movement.

**Temporal devices found in this search**
- Wrist-worn vibrotactile encodings that present clock time (Afzal, Hussain, Zhou, Prattichizzo, & Seneviratne, "Sensory substitution device for time presentation" [P]).
- Wrist-worn tactile stimuli used to manipulate subjective duration via the filled-duration illusion (*Sensors*, 2022, doi:10.3390/s22197194 [P]).
- A multimodal haptic vest from the EU ChronoPilot project, aimed at modulating subjective time experience [P].

**My interpretation.** The temporal devices found either transmit symbolic time (what time it is) or alter felt duration. None reported conveying the rate or relationship of slow external processes as a learned sensory channel. This is an absence in a limited search, not proof of a gap.

**Key disanalogy (my interpretation).** Sensorimotor contingency theory ties perceptual learning to lawful relations between the user's own action and sensory change. Slow external processes do not respond to the observer's action on short timescales. Whether an action-independent signal can support the same kind of learning is an open question that bears directly on whether "extended temporal perception" is achievable.

### 1.5 External cognition and visual representations of temporal relationships

**Larkin, J. H., & Simon, H. A. (1987).** *Cognitive Science*, 11(1), 65–99 [V]; **Scaife, M., & Rogers, Y. (1996).** *IJHCS*, 45, 185–213 [V] (both from prior scan)
- Theory: informationally equivalent representations differ in computational cost; external representations offload, re-represent and constrain.
- Relevance: the default explanation for any advantage of condition C is computational offloading. The pilot must be designed so this can be detected, not so it is mistaken for perception.

**Cleveland, W. S., McGill, M. E., & McGill, R. (1988).** The shape parameter of a two-variable graph. *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 83, 289–300 [V]
- Finding (theoretical, experimental and empirical): the aspect ratio of a graph affects the accuracy of slope judgements; banking to 45 degrees is proposed to maximise slope resolution.
- Relevance: in condition C, aspect ratio is a hidden manipulation of salience. It must be fixed and reported.

**Javed, W., McDonnel, B., & Elmqvist, N. (2010).** Graphical perception of multiple time series. *IEEE TVCG*, 16(6), 927–934. doi:10.1109/TVCG.2010.162 [P]
- Design: compared line graph techniques for comparison, slope and discrimination tasks with multiple series.
- Results not read in full; superimposed versus juxtaposed layout is a known design variable.

**Haroz, S., Kosara, R., & Franconeri, S. L. (2016).** The connected scatterplot for presenting paired time series. *IEEE TVCG*, 22(9), 2174–2186. doi:10.1109/TVCG.2015.2502587 [V]
- Finding (direct): low-complexity connected scatterplots can be understood with little explanation; viewers preferentially inspected them; specific misinterpretations were documented.
- Relevance: an existing representation designed for exactly the relation between two time series.

**Harrison, L., Yang, F., Franconeri, S., & Chang, R. (2014).** Ranking visualizations of correlation using Weber's law. *IEEE TVCG*, 20(12), 1943–1952. doi:10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346979 [V]
- Finding (direct, n = 1,687): precision of correlation judgements across nine visualisations could be modelled by Weber's law, with large differences between positive and negative correlation.
- **Kay, M., & Heer, J. (2016).** Beyond Weber's law: A second look at ranking visualizations of correlation. *IEEE TVCG* (InfoVis 2015) [P]. Reanalysis found the data deviate from Weber's law; scatterplots combined low individual variance with high precision.
- **Yang, F., Harrison, L. T., Rensink, R. A., Franconeri, S. L., & Chang, R. (2018).** Correlation judgment and visualization features: A comparative study. IEEE VIS [P]. Supports the hypothesis that people use a small number of visual features as proxies for correlation.
- Relevance: "seeing a relationship" in a chart may be reading a proxy feature. This is the core risk for condition C.

**Correll, M., & Heer, J. (2017).** Regression by eye: Estimating trends in bivariate visualizations. *Proc. CHI 2017*. doi:10.1145/3025453.3025922 [V]
- Finding (direct, crowdsourced): viewers estimated trends accurately in many standard charts, but visual features and outliers produced systematic deviations from least-squares estimates.

**Isenberg, P., Bezerianos, A., Dragicevic, P., & Fekete, J.-D. (2011).** A study on dual-scale data charts. *IEEE TVCG*, 17(12), 2469–2478. doi:10.1109/TVCG.2011.160 [V]
- Finding (direct): for charts mixing two resolutions (focus and context), cut-out charts performed best and superimposed focus/context designs should be avoided.
- Caution: this study is often cited against dual-axis charts of two different variables. It tested dual-scale focus+context charts, which is a different design. I cite it only for its actual finding.

**Visual analytics for lagged relationships** [P]
- Windowed cross-correlation visual analytics for comparing time series ensembles (Köthur et al., *Computer Graphics Forum*, 2015, doi:10.1111/cgf.12653).
- Ikat plots for stable cross-impact patterns in bivariate time series (Andrienko et al., *IEEE TVCG*, 2026, doi:10.1109/tvcg.2026.3676810).
- Relevance: tools that compute lag relationships explicitly already exist for analysts. They compute the relationship; they do not test whether a viewer recognises it unaided.

## Part 2. Challenging the research premise

### 2.1 Do existing instruments already provide the capability?

For recorded data, largely yes at the level of analysis:

| Capability | Existing instrument | What it does | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detect that a slow change occurred | Before/after comparison, difference images, anomaly charts | Restores a transient or fixed reference | Whether this is perception or comparison |
| See order and lag between two processes | Aligned line charts, windowed cross-correlation, cross-impact plots | Makes order spatial or computes it | Whether viewers read it accurately and how |
| See co-variation | Scatterplots, connected scatterplots | Encodes relation as shape | Proxy-feature reading; misinterpretation |
| See slow change unfold | Time-lapse | Brings rate into visible range | Speed and duration biases; relational limits |
| Feel a continuous non-native signal | Sensory augmentation (spatial) | Learned sensorimotor coupling | Not shown for slow external temporal relations |

**My interpretation.** For data already recorded, the capability to *expose* a relationship between two slow changes exists. The open questions are (a) how well people recognise the relationship from these representations, (b) which property of the representation does the work, and (c) whether anything about this deserves the word perception.

### 2.2 Six levels of improvement

| Level | Operational test | Evidence that would show it |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory perception | Change in sensitivity to the stimulus property itself | Lower threshold without the instrument, after training; adaptation-like effects |
| Attention | More of the relevant signal selected | Improvement removed by matching attentional cueing |
| Memory | Earlier states retained or externalised | Improvement removed when memory load is equated |
| Relational comprehension | Correct judgement of the relation, not just its components | Improvement in relation given both components detected |
| Decision accuracy | Correct response | Can rise through bias or heuristics alone |
| Subjective experience | Reports of "seeing" the relation | Can rise without accuracy |

These do not follow from each other. A pilot that reports only decision accuracy cannot locate the effect.

### 2.3 Terminology

Two candidate terms, held open:

- **Extended temporal perception.** Defensible only if an instrument changes sensitivity in ways that show perceptual signatures. Candidate criteria (my interpretation, drawing on the perception versus cognition debate reviewed by Firestone and Phillips, *Science*, 2023, 379(6638), 1196 [P]): judgements become fast and relatively automatic; robust under cognitive load; subject to perceptual phenomena such as adaptation; and, following König et al., acquired through extended coupled use. No study found meets these for slow temporal relations.
- **Instrument-mediated temporal cognition.** Describes what the existing evidence supports: representations change the computational cost and accuracy of temporal judgements (Larkin and Simon; Liersch and McKenzie; Hullman et al.).

**Recommendation:** use "instrument-mediated temporal recognition" as the working description of what the pilot measures, and reserve "extended perception" for a separately specified hypothesis with its own criteria. This does not resolve the terminology for the programme.

## Part 3. Candidate temporal relationships

### 3.1 Comparison matrix

| ID | Relationship | Underlying limitation | Experimentally demonstrated? | Existing instruments | Unresolved | Possible test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Onset order** of two gradual changes: which began first | No transient at gradual onset (attention); order judgement limited by noisy temporal coding; onset judgements contaminated by offsets | Components yes (gradual change blindness; TOJ for millisecond-scale rise times). Not for multi-second onsets | Aligned line charts; time-lapse; cross-correlation | Unaided performance for slow onsets; whether representations expose order or only detection | 3-condition onset-order task (Part 4) |
| R2 | **Lead–lag** between two co-varying slow processes | Same as R1 plus need to bind features across processes (slow binding limit) | Binding limits yes for fast stimuli (Holcombe; Fujisaki and Nishida). Not for slow drifts | Windowed cross-correlation; cross-impact plots; connected scatterplots | Whether compression helps or crosses binding limits | Lag sign judgement under compression factors |
| R3 | **Co-variation** of two gradual changes, each individually unnoticed | Detection failure for each component; correlation reading via proxy features | Correlation perception in static charts yes (Harrison et al.; Kay and Heer). Not for unfolding slow processes | Scatterplots; connected scatterplots | Whether co-variation can be recognised when components are not detected | Covary versus independent discrimination |
| R4 | **Rate change** (acceleration) of a slow drift | Lower sensitivity to acceleration than speed (contested) | For visible motion yes, contested (Watamaniuk and Heinen; Nakayama and Motoyoshi). Not for slow drift | Line charts; derivative plots | Whether representation of rate (derivative plot) beats raw trajectory | Constant versus accelerating drift discrimination |
| R5 | **Cumulative magnitude** (area or duration under a trajectory) | Peak-end summarising, duration neglect | Yes for retrospective evaluation; graphs can remove it (Liersch and McKenzie) | Line graphs; filled area | Generalisation beyond hedonic episodes | Total-exposure comparison across formats |
| R6 | **Drift from a fixed baseline** | Reference drift with exposure (normalisation) | Observational evidence (PNAS 2019); essay (Pauly). Controlled evidence not found | Anomaly charts against fixed periods | Criterion shift versus sensitivity | Anchored remarkability with SDT |
| R7 | **Synchrony of turning points** (do two processes reverse together) | Relational timing limits | Fast-stimulus synchrony limits yes; slow not found | Aligned charts | As R2 | Synchronous versus offset reversal discrimination |

### 3.2 Choice of candidate

**Recommended: R1, onset order of two gradual changes.**

Reasons:
1. **Invariance under compression.** Order is preserved by uniform time scaling and by mapping time to space. Speed and duration distortions documented for time-lapse (Steinhof et al., 2025) do not bias the correct answer. Conditions A, B and C can therefore be information-equivalent for the judged property.
2. **Grounded limitation, unknown magnitude.** Gradual change blindness predicts that onsets produce no attention-capturing transient. Whether informed observers can nevertheless order slow onsets is not established in sources found. Condition A therefore measures something genuinely unknown.
3. **Clean controls.** Simultaneous, single-change and no-change trials provide false-positive measures. Chance levels are defined.
4. **Separable stages.** Detection and order can be measured separately, so an instrument that only restores detection can be distinguished from one that exposes the relation.
5. **Independence from institutions.** Any two slow processes qualify.

R2 is the natural follow-up; it adds binding and makes a sharper prediction about compression (non-monotonic effect).

## Part 4. Recommended experimental protocol (pilot)

### 4.1 Purpose and claims

- Purpose: test feasibility, estimate effect sizes and variance, detect floor and ceiling problems.
- Not a test of perceptual extension. No inferential claims beyond feasibility.

### 4.2 Hypotheses (for later confirmatory work, stated now to constrain design)

- H1: In condition A, order accuracy for slow onsets is low, even when both changes are reported.
- H2: Conditions B and C improve order accuracy relative to A.
- H3 (diagnostic): Any improvement in C relative to B reflects offloading of the trajectory into space, testable by the endpoint control (4.9).

### 4.3 Stimuli

- Two discs, left and right of a central fixation dot, on a mid-grey background. Size and position scaled to viewport; minimum disc diameter 100 px.
- Changing feature: lightness. Start value identical for both discs. Changing discs ramp linearly by a fixed magnitude (initial value: ±12 units in CIELAB L*, approximated in sRGB; direction randomised per disc).
- Real-time trial duration T = 30 s.
- Ramp duration R = 15 s, **identical for both discs**, so offset asynchrony equals onset asynchrony. (Jaśkowski's offset effect cannot be eliminated this way; it is controlled as a confound in 4.6.)
- First onset at a random time between 3 and 8 s. Asynchrony Δ ∈ {0, 2, 4, 7} s, leader side counterbalanced. All ramps end before T.
- Values sampled at 10 Hz into a stored array per trial. All conditions render from the same array.

### 4.4 Conditions (between-subjects)

- **A. Continuous observation.** The 30 s sequence plays in real time, rendered with interpolation between samples.
- **B. Time-compressed replay.** The same array played at 10x (3 s). Asynchronies become 0, 0.2, 0.4 and 0.7 s. Plays once per trial. No looping (a loop reset would produce an abrupt flicker-like transient that reveals change).
- **C. Static temporal representation.** Two line traces of the same arrays, stacked vertically with a shared time axis (0 to 30 s), identical y-scales, fixed aspect ratio, light vertical gridlines every 5 s, no onset markers, no labels beyond "Left" and "Right". Visible for up to 30 s or until response.

Between-subjects assignment prevents carry-over: once participants have seen C, they know what the changes look like.

### 4.5 Instructions and calibration

- All conditions: participants are told that either disc may change slowly, or not at all. Intentional instructions remove the "unexpected change" component and make the test conservative.
- Calibration (all conditions): static start and end discs shown side by side; participant must report the difference on at least 9 of 10 trials. This establishes that the total change is suprathreshold on that display, so failure in A cannot be attributed to an invisible total change.
- Practice: two trials with abrupt changes to learn the response format. No feedback on main trials.

### 4.6 Independent and dependent variables

**Independent variables**
- Condition (A, B, C), between-subjects.
- Asynchrony |Δ| (0, 2, 4, 7 s real time), within-subjects.
- Trial type (both change, one changes, none changes), within-subjects.

**Dependent variables (per trial)**
1. Change report for each disc (yes/no).
2. If both reported: order judgement (left first, right first, same time).
3. Confidence (1 to 4).
4. Response time from end of presentation.
5. Logged frame timestamps (requestAnimationFrame) for timing audit.

### 4.7 Trial structure and randomisation

24 main trials per participant:
- 12 asynchronous both-change trials: 3 asynchrony levels × 2 leader sides × 2 repetitions.
- 4 simultaneous both-change trials (Δ = 0).
- 4 single-change trials (2 left, 2 right).
- 4 no-change trials.

Randomisation:
- Trial order randomised per participant, seeded and logged.
- Leader side, change direction and first-onset time randomised within constraints.
- Condition assignment randomised at entry.
- Disc positions fixed (left and right) to keep the response mapping simple.

### 4.8 Outcome measures and analysis

**Primary**
- Conditional order accuracy: proportion correct on asynchronous trials where both changes were reported.
- Unconditional relational success: both changes reported and order correct.
- Detection sensitivity d′ per condition, using single-change and no-change trials for false alarms.

**Bias and false positives**
- "Same time" rate on simultaneous trials (false order reports).
- False "both changed" rate on single-change and no-change trials.
- Order response bias (proportion left-first independent of truth).

**Secondary**
- Confidence–accuracy relation (does C raise confidence more than accuracy?).
- Order accuracy as a function of |Δ| per condition; threshold estimates only if data allow.
- Completion time, dropout, calibration failure, timing jitter.

**Analysis approach**
- Descriptive estimates with bootstrap confidence intervals by condition and asynchrony.
- Exploratory mixed-effects logistic regression: order correct ~ condition × |Δ| + (1 | participant).
- Signal detection measures (d′, criterion c) for change reports.
- Pre-register the analysis plan before collecting pilot data. Treat all pilot statistics as estimates for power analysis.

**Pilot size:** about 12 participants per condition (36 total). Sufficient to check floor and ceiling and estimate variance; not sufficient for inference.

**Feasibility criteria (proposed)**
- Condition A order accuracy (conditional) below ceiling and above floor at the largest |Δ|.
- B and C not at ceiling at the smallest non-zero |Δ|.
- Median session under 25 minutes.
- Frame timing deviations within a stated tolerance on at least 90% of trials.

### 4.9 Recommended control arm (strongly advised, optional for the first pilot)

- **D. Endpoint comparison.** Static start and end states of both discs. Because both ramps end at the same level after the same ramp duration, endpoints reveal *whether* each disc changed but not *which began first*.
- Prediction: D supports detection at C's level but order at chance.
- If D matches C on order, the representation is leaking order through a feature other than the trajectory (a design fault).
- If D matches C on detection but not order, C's order advantage cannot be attributed to detection offloading alone.

### 4.10 Browser implementation constraints (no backend)

- Single static HTML page; all stimuli generated client-side from a seeded generator.
- Data saved locally and offered to the participant as a downloadable JSON or CSV file at the end, for return by a separate channel.
- Record user agent, viewport size, device pixel ratio and frame timing.
- Uncontrolled displays mean absolute luminance is unknown; the calibration step and within-condition comparisons are the protection. Results do not generalise to calibrated psychophysics.

### 4.11 Confounds

| Confound | How it biases | Mitigation in this design | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attention / vigilance | A demands 30 s sustained watching per trial; fatigue lowers A | Intentional instructions; breaks every 6 trials; record time-on-task | A may fail through vigilance rather than temporal limits |
| Working memory | A and B require holding earlier states; C does not | Endpoint arm D separates detection offloading | C's order advantage may still be memory offloading of the trajectory |
| Display salience | C encodes change as spatial slope (a different channel from lightness) | Fixed aspect ratio; gridlines identical; no markers | Slope reading may be easier for reasons unrelated to time |
| Exposure time | A 30 s, B 3 s, C up to 30 s | Logged; exposure-matched B variant in follow-up | B disadvantaged by short exposure, or advantaged by brevity |
| Task difficulty | Judgement differs in kind across conditions (watch versus read) | Same response format and trial types | Conditions may test different tasks under one label |
| Offset cue | Observers may judge by offsets (Jaśkowski) | Equal ramp durations make offset order equal onset order | Cannot distinguish onset-based from offset-based judgement in this pilot |
| Demand characteristics | C looks like "the instrument"; participants may infer the hypothesis | Between-subjects; neutral framing; catch trials | Higher effort in C |
| Response bias | Tendency to report "ordered" rather than "same" | Simultaneous and single-change catch trials; criterion estimates | Some bias inseparable at pilot sample sizes |
| Display variability | Uncontrolled monitors, gamma, ambient light | Calibration step; record device data | Between-participant noise |

## Part 5. Adversarial evaluation

### 5.1 Three strongest routes to a misleading positive result

**1. Computational offloading presented as relational recognition.**
Condition C shows the whole history at once. The order judgement becomes a static spatial comparison (which trace departs from flat first), which people do well. A C advantage would then show that a graph is easier than memory, a result known since Larkin and Simon. Visual-feature proxy research (Yang et al.) adds that viewers may read a single local feature, not the relationship.

**2. Compression restores detection, and detection is mistaken for relational recognition.**
At 10x, gradual changes may become visible as ordinary change. If order accuracy in B rises only because more trials now have both changes detected, the unconditional success rate improves without any improvement in judging the relation.

**3. Bias and demand inflate accuracy.**
Participants in B and C may respond "ordered" more often and with more confidence, which raises hits on asynchronous trials if they also have a weak cue. Combined with demand characteristics, this can produce an apparent effect without improved sensitivity.

### 5.2 Evidence that distinguishes genuine improvement from simpler explanations

| Alternative explanation | Distinguishing evidence |
|---|---|
| Offloading / easier comparison | C advantage over D on order but not on detection; C advantage persists when trajectories include noise that defeats a single-feature proxy (follow-up) |
| Detection restoration | Improvement in *conditional* order accuracy (given both changes reported), not only in unconditional success |
| Response bias | Improvement in sensitivity measures with stable criterion; low "ordered" rates on simultaneous trials; confidence rising no faster than accuracy |
| Exposure differences | Effect survives an exposure-matched B variant |
| Vigilance failure in A | A performance stable across the session; A deficit present on early trials |

### 5.3 Findings that would falsify or substantially weaken the hypothesis

1. **No unaided limitation.** If condition A, with intentional instructions, shows high conditional order accuracy, slow onset order is not difficult for informed observers. The premise fails for R1: the problem is noticing unexpected change, not recognising the relation.
2. **No relational gain.** If B and C improve detection but not conditional order accuracy, the instruments restore an existing capacity and expose nothing new about the relationship.
3. **Endpoint equivalence.** If D matches C on order, the representation is not exposing a temporal relation; the design leaks order through another feature.
4. **Bias only.** If gains appear in criterion but not sensitivity, or in confidence but not accuracy, the effect is in decision, not recognition.
5. **No compression limit (follow-up, R2).** If relational accuracy keeps improving with compression far beyond binding-rate limits, the slow-limit account does not apply, weakening any perceptual interpretation of B.

A positive pilot would still show only instrument-mediated recognition. Extended perception would require separate evidence meeting the criteria in 2.3.

## Part 6. Unresolved scientific questions

1. What is the psychometric function for detecting gradual change as a function of rate and magnitude under intentional viewing?
2. Can informed observers order onsets of changes that rise over seconds to tens of seconds, and what cue do they use (onset, midpoint, offset)?
3. Does time compression improve relational judgements up to a limit and then degrade them, as slow binding limits would predict?
4. When a static trace exposes order, are viewers reading the relationship or a local proxy feature?
5. Can co-variation between two processes be recognised when neither component change is detected?
6. Can an action-independent signal about slow external processes be learned as a sensorimotor contingency, or does perceptual learning require the observer's own action?
7. Do instrument-mediated gains transfer to unaided observation after training, or vanish when the instrument is removed?
8. What criteria would the programme accept as evidence of perceptual, rather than cognitive, change?

## Part 8. Scan limitations

- Most entries rely on abstracts, author pages or secondary listings rather than full texts. Designs and effect sizes should be confirmed before citing.
- Search was targeted, not systematic. Absences (no slow-onset order studies, no temporal augmentation for slow relations, no gradual-change rate threshold function) are scan findings only.
- Not searched: source monitoring; perceptual learning without action; animated versus static comparisons specifically for time series; temporal binding windows in vision at multi-second scales.
