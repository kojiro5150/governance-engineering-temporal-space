# Temporal Space in Institutional Reasoning

**An exploratory inquiry within Governance Engineering**

- Author: Sam Hayward, Governance Engineering
- Recorded: 9 October 2026
- Version: 0.1, Exploratory Foundation
- Status: Exploratory. Not validated. Not a paper or implementation specification. No PHDSS code or contracts changed.
- Origin: An extended conceptual exploration conducted across two AI systems, synthesised here as a permanent record.

---

## 1. Purpose of this record

This document records an open inquiry into time as a "fourth dimension" of institutional reasoning. It preserves the ideas, distinctions, corrections, lineage and open questions so the inquiry can continue without being collapsed prematurely into a single feature, module or paper.

The inquiry is deliberately held open. Controls and studies described here are experiments inside it, not its boundary.

---

## 2. The core hypothesis

### Organising question

Can we extend human temporal perception so that people can recognise relationships across time (between what happened, what might happen, what could have happened, and how others experience those trajectories) that are otherwise difficult or impossible to apprehend together?

Governance Engineering asks what this expanded perception means for institutional power, participation, authority and decision-space formation. PHDSS asks how selected aspects might be represented, examined and evaluated without confusing evidence, interpretation, imagination or participation.

### Two independent research streams

1. **Human Temporal Perception (primary exploratory ambition):** can an external instrument extend our capacity to perceive duration, change, branching, relationships and multiple temporal perspectives?
2. **Institutional Temporal Reasoning:** can those extended capabilities improve how institutions understand and govern decisions and their consequences?

The first stream remains free to develop even if the second proves unsuccessful.

**Next question (carried into v0.2):** what is the smallest temporal relationship a human cannot readily perceive unaided, but could reliably recognise through an appropriately designed instrument?

### The governance framing

Institutional reasoning happens in a constrained space: options, evidence and perspectives examined from the present moment, then narrowed further by deadlines, roles, evidence hierarchies and power.

Adding time as a fourth dimension of **perception** lets reasoning:

- move **back** to how a problem, constraint or decision formed
- move **forward** into possible futures
- move **sideways** across branches not yet closed
- move **across** into other people's lived experience of time

That movement may reveal problems, possibilities and inequalities that are invisible from the present-only frame.

We cannot control time or physically travel through it. We may be able to change how we move through it: what we attend to, how far we look, when we close possibilities, how we interpret the past and which future we steer toward.

### The aspirational hypothesis (broader than governance)

External representations and instruments can extend human temporal perception beyond what unaided cognition can sustain. Writing, clocks, geology, time-lapse photography and the Long Now clock each did this before. There may be a candidate gap in existing instruments at the scale of institutions and lived consequences: years to decades, with processes too slow and too distributed to see. Whether current approaches already provide much of this capability is for the literature and experimental work to establish.

### The governance hypothesis (one application)

Institutions can use such capabilities to recognise obscured decision histories, temporal inequalities, alternative futures and consequential changes in what remains possible.

The first hypothesis is broader. The second is a testing ground. PHDSS may become one place to investigate the second without defining the limits of the first.

## 3. The arc of the inquiry

1. **Temporal decision space.** Decision space expands through time as well as across perspectives, evidence and alternatives.
2. **Fact, memory, history.** Commitment makes a decision fact. Memory (what the institution retains of its reasoning) is not automatic. History (interpretation) keeps changing. Most institutional failure sits between fact and memory.
3. **Branching time.** The past is a fixed trunk; the future is a tree of possibilities. Commitment selects a branch. Unchosen branches become counterfactuals worth remembering.
4. **Premature convergence and delayed commitment.** Closing branches before exploration, and losing branches by waiting. Between them sits a defensible window, not a single optimal moment.
5. **How people move through time.** Chronos and kairos, construal level, mental time travel, temporal asymmetry.
6. **Temporal agency and justice.** Who controls when possibilities close, and who lives with the consequences.
7. **Institutional situatedness and incompleteness.** Institutions reason from inside time, never above it.
8. **Flatland.** Accurate observations inside incomplete interpretations; missing dimensions are conceptual before informational; institutions suppress those who report them.
9. **The tesseract.** Slice versus projection. Every representation preserves some properties and compresses others.
10. **Spacetime.** Position and timing are two shadows of one decision position. No universal institutional "now". Light cones as the live decision space.
11. **Geometry of power.** Power may curve the decision space rather than push decisions. Decisions drift downhill toward the powerful while everyone experiences straight-line reasoning.
12. **Governance Engineering's object.** Decision-space formation: the architecture that makes some decisions possible and others not.
13. **Widening the present.** Augustine's three presents. Institutions hold memory, attention and expectation in fragments across people.
14. **Emergent decisions.** Some of the most consequential decisions are never formally made.
15. **Formal versus effective commitment.** Options can become infeasible without an authorised decision. A decision becomes historical fact only when it is made.
16. **Temporal capability.** Institutions shape how others experience time and how far they can move within it.
17. **Extending human temporal perception.** A navigable temporal space, not a timeline.

## 4. Key concepts

### 4.1 Movements in perceived time

| Direction | Mode | Epistemic status |
|---|---|---|
| Back | Reconstruction | Evidence (partial, reshaped by hindsight) |
| Forward | Imagination | Hypothesis (never fact) |
| Sideways | Counterfactual / branching | Possibility (modality) |
| Across | Other people's timelines | Only reachable through their participation |

We cannot strictly perceive past or future. We reconstruct and imagine. Each needs a different remedy: records, structured exploration, and the people themselves.

### 4.2 The decision lifecycle

- **Possibility:** an alternative remains genuinely available.
- **Effective commitment:** actions or circumstances have substantially constrained alternatives.
- **Formal commitment:** an authorised human explicitly chooses.
- **Realised outcome:** consequences actually occur.
- **Historical interpretation:** the decision is documented, contested and reinterpreted.

These are not necessarily sequential. Effective commitment is not legitimate commitment. Making a decision actualises the choice, not the predicted future.

**Temporal reality versus temporal representation.** To avoid equating institutional recognition with the existence of reality:

- A decision becomes historical fact when it is made.
- An option can become infeasible without an authorised decision.
- A commitment changes obligations and available possibilities.
- An outcome becomes historical fact when it occurs, not when it is documented.

Three statements with different statuses after commitment:

| Statement | Status |
|---|---|
| "We decided A on date X" | Historical fact |
| "A was the best decision" | Contestable |
| "A will achieve its aims" | Empirical question |

### 4.3 Three constructs of temporal stewardship

| Construct | Question | Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Temporal opportunity | Was there opportunity to explore? | Premature convergence (or delayed commitment) |
| Temporal cognition | How did proximity to commitment change reasoning? | Values drift, tunnelling |
| Temporal justice | Who bears consequences, and who had influence? | Temporal asymmetry |

### 4.4 Temporal breadth, integration and agency

- **Breadth:** how much past and possible future is considered.
- **Integration:** how far those perspectives actually influence present reasoning.
- **Agency:** who decides which perspectives count and when commitments bind.

Institutions often hold all three of Augustine's presents (memory, attention, expectation) but fragmented across people. The clinician holds the memory, the executive the attention, the consumer the expectation. Temporal incompleteness often comes from fragmentation, not absence.

### 4.5 Temporal agency

The practical ability to influence the timing, sequencing, duration and reversibility of consequential decisions. Distinct from temporal horizon (how far someone looks) and temporal exposure (how long they live with consequences). The person most exposed often has the least agency.

Institutions do not only perceive time from a constrained position. They manufacture the temporal positions of others: waiting lists, consultation windows, deadlines.

### 4.6 Slice, project, rotate

| Operation | Question | PHDSS analogue |
|---|---|---|
| Slice | What was knowable at this moment? | Governance Record at commitment |
| Project | What does the accumulated record show? | Decision Surface |
| Rotate | What changes from another frame? | Director perspectives / frame switching |
| Branch | What else remained possible? | Alternatives preserved at commitment |

Every projection preserves some properties and compresses others. What is preserved is a design choice.

### 4.7 The light-cone mapping (analogy)

- Past cone: evidence that could have reached the decision in time.
- Future cone: options still reachable.
- "Elsewhere": what the institution can neither know nor influence from where it stands.

Correction: in governance, "elsewhere" is usually not unreachable. People outside the cone were often excluded or never asked. Participation is how an institution reaches its elsewhere.

### 4.8 Availability, recognition, actionability

For any concern, evidence or alternative:

- **Availability:** when could it first have informed the decision?
- **Recognition:** when was it actually acknowledged as relevant?
- **Actionability:** until when could it materially change the available choices?

Core test: did recognition fall inside the actionability window?

Restated in Augustine's terms: **can knowledge from another time still change what we decide now?**

### 4.9 Geometry of the decision space

Four structural properties shape which decisions emerge:

- Epistemic structure: which knowledge is recognised and usable.
- Authority structure: who can introduce, challenge, defer or commit.
- Temporal structure: when alternatives become available, costly or irreversible.
- Representational structure: what the framework makes visible.

Power may act as curvature rather than force. Prominence bias is a power artefact: graphs built from documents favour what is densely documented, and documentation density follows institutional power.

### 4.10 Three levels of Governance Engineering

| Level | Object | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Decision analysis | The decision | Was it adequately reasoned? |
| Decision-space analysis | Available possibilities | What could decision-makers recognise and influence? |
| Decision-space formation | The architecture producing possibilities | What determines which possibilities become visible, credible, feasible and actionable? |

The third level is interventionist and is the distinctive object of Governance Engineering.

Reflexive requirement: engineering the decision space is distinct from **governing the engineering**. Whoever designs the space holds power over it. Lived experience governance committees are a working precedent for governing the engineering.

Candidate refined definition: *Governance Engineering is the systematic design, evaluation and accountable modification of the institutional structures through which evidence, participation, authority and time shape the formation, consideration and commitment of decisions.* (To be reconciled with the published GE discipline paper as a refinement, not a replacement.)

Core distinction: *Decision support improves reasoning within an existing decision space. Governance Engineering examines and redesigns the conditions that create that space.*

### 4.11 Emergent decisions

Some consequential decisions are never formally made. A service disappears through defensible operational changes and nobody believes they closed it. Procurement specifications exclude alternatives months before formal approval.

Measure: the gap between **effective commitment date** (when alternatives actually disappeared) and **formal authorisation date**.

### 4.12 Every constraint has a date

Constraints that appear absolute in one slice usually have temporal edges: when they were built, when they open (renewal, budget cycle, review), and whether they are eroding or hardening. The fourth-dimensional move is asking when the wall was built and when it opens, not breaking through it.

### 4.13 Perception versus capability

Institutions are often limited less by what they can do than by what they can perceive from their position in time, frame and power. Expanding perception is cheaper than expanding capability and comes first.

Boundaries: some limits are real (workforce, funding). "It's a mindset problem" is a familiar way to deny resource shortfalls. Over time, perceptual limits convert into real ones: missing an option leads to committing elsewhere, and the option disappears.

### 4.14 Holding perspectives unmerged

Premature convergence happens to accounts as well as futures. The capacity to hold several partially incompatible temporal accounts at once, without collapsing them into one authoritative version, may be a core capability. Invariants (dates, evidence, events) give common ground; significance, adequacy and responsibility can remain plural.

### 4.15 Visibility, comprehension, agency

| Level | Capability | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Temporal visibility | Make otherwise hard-to-observe relationships accessible | What can I now see? |
| Temporal comprehension | Understand relationships, uncertainty and significance | What can I now understand? |
| Temporal agency | Use that understanding to influence reachable futures | What can I now do differently? |

These levels do not follow automatically from one another. An instrument might improve visibility while increasing confusion, or improve comprehension without increasing anyone's freedom to act. Those would be findings, not failures of the inquiry.

### 4.16 Time geography as the closest foundation

Hägerstrand's space-time paths, prisms, and capability, coupling and authority constraints are the most rigorous existing basis for temporal reachability, without relying on quantum analogies. This inquiry may extend beyond time geography by asking how an instrument could let people perceive and navigate multiple trajectories, alternative futures and lived temporal experiences. That difference is to be examined, not claimed as novel.

## 5. Principles and candidate invariants

1. **Analytical representation is not participation.** (Existing PHDSS invariant.)
2. **Analytical projection of future consequences is not representation by those who will live with them.**
3. **Completeness within a framework is not completeness of the framework.** A Decision Ledger can pass every structural test and faithfully represent an inadequate model.
4. **The absence of a recorded possibility is not evidence that it did not exist.**
5. **Representational prominence is not substantive importance.**
6. **Consequential narrowing should require authorship.** Where actions materially narrow the decision space, the narrowing should be visible, attributable and contestable while alternatives remain.
7. **Observed, interpreted and imagined must never blur.** A modelled future never carries the status of a documented event.
8. **Wider temporal perspective is not more accurate perspective.** It can introduce speculation and hindsight bias.
9. **PHDSS is not the Sphere.** It is another bounded instrument and must not claim a privileged vantage point.

Three tiers of completeness for the Decision Surface:

- Structural completeness: fields and links present (machine-checkable).
- Evidentiary adequacy: claims sufficiently supported (judgement).
- Frame adequacy: representation fits the decision (judgement and participation).

The verifier should state that it certifies structural completeness only.

## 6. The physics analogy lab

Physics is a source of questions, not foundations. Each analogy records what it suggests and where it breaks.

| Concept | What it suggests | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Spacetime (Minkowski) | Position and timing are aspects of one decision position | Institutions do not obey spacetime geometry |
| Relativity of simultaneity | No universal institutional "now" | Perspectives are not equivalent inertial frames; they differ in power and legitimacy |
| Invariants | The commitment event is the shared fact across frames | Governance has no mathematical invariants |
| Light cones | Live decision space = what can be known and still acted on | Exclusion is not causal unreachability |
| Curvature (general relativity) | Power shapes the space; decisions drift without pressure | People deliberate and resist; they are not particles on geodesics |
| Branching time (Prior) | Formal structure for open futures and fixed past | Branches are modelled possibilities, not physical timelines |
| Entropy / arrow of time | Why the past leaves records and the future does not | Entropy is connected to, not a complete explanation of, memory asymmetry; it gives time direction, not constancy |
| Flatland | Accurate slices, incomplete interpretation; suppression of those who see more | The Square is the challenger, not the failure; the Circles are the failure |
| Tesseract | Slice versus projection; rotation as reframing | No projection preserves all relationships |
| Superposition (provocation) | Is an undecided decision uncertain, or undetermined until commitment? | Decisions are not quantum states |
| Measurement (provocation) | Does naming a problem change how the institution treats it? | Quantum measurement is physical interaction, not human noticing |
| Entanglement (provocation) | Can one trajectory be understood apart from those bound to it? | Interdependence and coupled systems are the accurate concepts |

Not every analogy must produce a control. Discovering where an analogy fails is part of the value.

### Corrections made during the inquiry

- Humans already exist in four-dimensional spacetime; the difference is that we cannot navigate time as we navigate space.
- Einstein initially dismissed Minkowski's geometric formulation, then embraced it; gravity as curvature belongs to general relativity, which Minkowski's geometry enabled.
- Entropy gives time its direction, not constancy. Relativity shows elapsed time varies by observer.
- In Flatland, the institutional failure is the Circles who imprisoned the Square, not the Square.
- The Sphere's dismissal of a fourth dimension shows that higher vantage points are also bounded.

## 7. Implications for PHDSS

The temporal layer mostly reinterprets existing architecture rather than adding to it.

**What already exists:** Directors generate branches. The Governance Record is a slice at commitment. The Decision Surface is a projection. The V3 authority chain (AI enlarge and interrogate; code constrain, validate and preserve; Chair organise and expose; human judge and decide) already separates exposure from determination.

**Candidate additions (experiments, not commitments):**

- **Branch record at commitment:** the chosen path, live alternatives, why each was set aside, what would make it preferable later.
- **Reopening conditions:** rejected branches become watchpoints.
- **Dated constraints:** origin, authority, review point and reopening conditions for each constraint treated as fixed.
- **Readiness exposure, not verdict:** surface what evidence is pending, who has not participated, what is lost by deciding now and what is lost by waiting. The human decides.
- **Frame challenges:** traceable propositions questioning whether evaluative categories are adequate. AI-generated candidates must be labelled as hypotheses for participant validation, never findings, and never closed by the model.
- **Synthesis question:** should synthesis preserve unmerged accounts, showing convergence and incompatibility, rather than one integrated reading?

**Cautions:**

- The 13 Directors are not 13 independent observers. They share a model and may share blind spots. Agreement across Directors is weaker evidence than it appears.
- PHDSS reasons only about decisions brought to it. Emergent decisions are a structural blind spot.
- Sequencing: the Decision Surface prose-extraction fidelity test comes before any temporal build. Snapshots and trajectory tracking would compound extraction error.
- Individual stakeholder trajectories carry consent, privacy and power risks. Attach trajectories to contributions and their influence, not to individuals' changing positions.

## 8. First engineering control: Option Closure Gate

Candidate home: the vendor challenge module of the digital health executive decision tool.

- **Exclusion register:** records which previously viable option was excluded, through what change, by whose authority, and whether recoverable.
- **Gate questions:** Did the specification eliminate a viable option? Is the exclusion supported by identifiable authority? Can it be recovered? Were affected perspectives considered before exclusion?
- **Threshold:** measure option viability, not size of change. Track whether each option remains viable; this also captures cumulative narrowing.
- **Requirements:** visibility, attribution, contestability. Recording an exclusion does not authorise it.
- **Unknowns:** administrative cost and effect on procurement speed are not yet shown. A possible negative finding: perfect documentation with no change in whose perspectives influence exclusion.

The gate is one branch of the inquiry. If it fails, the theory does not. If it works, it is one confirmation in one domain.

## 9. Research pathway

No further controls or modules at this stage. The next intellectually valuable step is investigating what it would mean to perceive temporal relationships differently, not merely to display more temporal information.

The steps below apply mainly to the Institutional Temporal Reasoning stream.

1. **Concept note** capturing the core frame.
2. **Literature scan:** anticipatory governance, temporal justice, procedural justice, epistemic injustice, organisational memory, time geography.
3. **Retrospective case: e-rostering implementation failures.** Reconstruct when alternatives became unavailable; compare effective and formal commitment dates; apply availability, recognition and actionability; test whether the pattern resembles decision-based evidence-making.
4. **Values drift study using document version histories:** do stated values stay connected to option assessment across drafts of business cases and board papers? Runnable now without new tooling.
5. **Portfolio study:** two years of decisions at one institution, coded for evidence sequencing, participation timing and reopening authority. Tests structural asymmetry, which a single case cannot.
6. **Demo protocol on the HITH ledger:** slice at commitment, accumulated projection, rotation to the consumer frame. Record what changes in prominence.
7. **Intervention study:** introduce the Option Closure Gate and evaluate recognition timing, justification quality, challenge opportunity, burden and decision quality.

### Testable hypotheses

- Prematurely convergent processes show narrower exploration than comparable processes with meaningful opportunity for inquiry.
- Proximity to commitment is associated, under identifiable conditions, with shifts from abstract values toward immediate constraints.
- Greater temporal asymmetry between exposure and participation is associated with weaknesses in legitimacy, accountability or outcomes. (Requires outcome measures independent of participation data to avoid circularity.)
- Two institutions reaching the same decision with the same final evidence can differ in governance quality because their trajectories differ.

## 10. Open questions (held deliberately unresolved)

1. **Perception:** what becomes visible when an institution examines a problem from another point in time?
2. **Other people's time:** can a reasoning architecture help an institution understand how others live through its decisions without pretending to stand in their place?
3. **Branching:** can futures be held open long enough to be explored without the institution freezing?
4. **Collective time:** can an institution hold more time than any member can alone, or does it usually compress what individuals already see?
5. **Physics:** which spacetime ideas reveal real structure in institutional reasoning, and which only decorate it?
6. **Temporal invariance:** what remains true about a problem when the temporal perspective changes?
7. **Plural accounts:** how many temporal perspectives can an institution hold simultaneously without collapsing them into one authoritative account?
8. **Temporal capability:** does an institution's decision expand or contract others' capability to move through their own time?
9. **Perceived versus actual agency:** how do we distinguish choices people feel they have from choices actually reachable?
10. **Visual language of time:** what form (river, tree, cube, something else) can hold slices, projections, rotations, branches and plural timelines at once?
11. **The instrument:** if you could watch a situation unfold like a time-lapse forest, with the branches that never happened and everyone's experience inside it, what would you understand that you cannot today?
12. **The foundational question:** what does an institution owe a future it cannot know but has the authority to shape?

### Central questions for papers and boards

- Can an institution make a procedurally legitimate decision at the wrong time?
- Who has the authority to close possibilities that others will have to live with?
- When someone describes a dimension our framework cannot hold, do we examine the framework or dismiss the person?
- Are we evaluating the decision, or the conditions that made this decision and not another possible?

## 12. Summary in one paragraph

Institutions reason from inside time, from incomplete positions, and usually from the present alone. They cannot control time, but they can widen their present: reconstructing how problems formed, exploring futures as labelled hypotheses, holding branches open until explored, and bringing in the lived time of those who will inherit the consequences. Decisions become fact when they are made, but options can become infeasible earlier, through drift no one owns. Power shapes the decision space like curvature, deciding whose time counts and who controls when futures close. Governance Engineering's object is the architecture that forms that space. PHDSS can make its limits visible without claiming to see the whole. Beyond governance lies a larger aspiration: an instrument that extends what the human mind can perceive of reality across time.
